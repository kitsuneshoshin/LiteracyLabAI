How to correct your child's writing without putting them off

A page covered in red pen can make a child decide they are bad at writing. Good feedback does the opposite: it shows them what already works and gives them one clear thing to try next.

Read it as a reader first

Before you look for mistakes, read the piece the way you would read a story or a letter. Then tell them what you noticed: a line that made you smile, a detail you could picture, a reason that convinced you.

Praise something specific

"Good job" tells a child nothing. "I could really see the dark kitchen when you wrote the floor creaked" tells them exactly what to do again. Quote their words back to them.

Choose one thing to improve

Pick the single change that would help most: adding a reason, starting sentences in different ways, or finishing with a clear ending. One step is something a child can actually do in the next piece. A list of ten corrections feels like failure.

Leave most spelling for later

Unless the piece is for a spelling test, pick two or three words to fix together and let the rest go. Children who worry about spelling often write shorter, safer sentences. You can come back to spelling in a separate, low-pressure way.

Let them fix it themselves

Ask them to reread the sentence aloud and see what they would change. Children often hear a missing full stop or a clumsy phrase when they read it out. When they find it themselves, it sticks.

Show a corrected copy

Seeing their own sentences polished teaches more than a rule in a textbook. Write out a corrected version of one paragraph beside theirs, keeping their ideas and voice, and let them compare.

Common questions

Should I correct every mistake?

No. Fixing everything at once is discouraging and hard to learn from. Choose one improvement and two or three words.

My child will not rewrite anything. What can I do?

Try rewriting only one sentence together. Small wins build the habit.
