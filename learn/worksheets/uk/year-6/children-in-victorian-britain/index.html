Children in Victorian Britain

An information text about children's lives in Victorian Britain, with eight questions on evidence, explaining and summarising.

Year 6 (ages 10 to 11, UK)Information textAbout 30 minutes12 marks300 words

Download the worksheet (PDF)Download the answers (PDF)

Read the passage

A time of change

Queen Victoria ruled Britain from 1837 to 1901. During this time, factories, railways and coal mines grew quickly, and many families moved from the countryside to the new towns and cities. Life could be exciting, but it was also very hard, especially for poor families. Rich factory owners grew wealthy, while many workers lived in crowded streets and had little money to spare. Families often shared a single room, and several children might sleep in one bed.

Children at work

Many poor children had to work to help their families earn enough to live. Some worked in cotton mills, where they crawled under busy machines to clear away fluff. Others worked in coal mines, opening heavy wooden doors in dark tunnels. Chimney sweeps sent small boys up narrow chimneys to clean away soot. These jobs were tiring and dangerous, and the children were often paid very little.

Changing the law

Not everyone accepted that this was right. Writers such as Charles Dickens described the lives of poor children in their books, and many readers were shocked. Reformers pressed Parliament to act, and gradually new laws were passed. In 1842, a law stopped women, and boys under ten, from working underground in coal mines. Other laws limited the hours that children could work in factories.

Going to school

Before this, many poor children never went to school at all. Some attended church or charity schools, where lessons were strict and children chanted facts by heart. They wrote on slates with chalk and were often punished for mistakes. In 1880, school became compulsory for children up to the age of ten, and over time the school leaving age rose.

Richer families

Children from wealthy families had a very different life. They often lived in large houses with servants. Many were taught at home by a governess until they were sent away to school.

Answer the questions

  1. Between which years did Queen Victoria rule Britain? (1 mark)
  2. What did children in cotton mills crawl under busy machines to do? (1 mark)
  3. Find and copy one word from the section called “Changing the law” that means very surprised and upset. (1 mark)
  4. Give two reasons why the law began to change. Use the text to help you. (2 marks)
  5. Choose one job from the section called “Children at work” and explain why it was dangerous for a child. (2 marks)
  6. Do you think the new laws came soon enough? Give a reason. (1 mark)
  7. How was life different for children from wealthy families? (2 marks)
  8. Write two sentences to sum up what life was like for children in Victorian Britain and how it began to change. (2 marks)
Answer key for parents and teachers

1. From 1837 to 1901. (1 mark)

Finding information (2b). A fact to find in the first section.

2. Clear away fluff. (1 mark)

Finding information (2b). Finding a detail in the right section.

3. shocked (1 mark)

Word meaning (2a). The child matches a word to its meaning, using the section heading to find it.

4. Writers such as Charles Dickens described the lives of poor children, and many readers were shocked; reformers also pressed Parliament to act. (2 marks)

Explaining from the text. The child picks out the causes that are spread across a short section.

5. Any one with a reason, for example: crawling under busy machines, where a child could be hurt, or climbing narrow chimneys, where a child could get stuck or breathe in soot. (2 marks)

Inference (2d). The text says the jobs were dangerous but does not explain how, so the child has to work it out.

6. Any view with a sensible reason, for example: No, because children had already been hurt or kept from school for many years. (1 mark)

Giving a view with evidence. There is no single right answer. What matters is a reason that fits the facts in the text.

7. They lived in large houses with servants, and many were taught at home by a governess before being sent away to school. (2 marks)

Explaining from the text. Two details in the last section have to be joined to answer the question.

8. One mark for the hard lives of poor children (work, little schooling) and one for the changes (new laws and school becoming compulsory), with a nod to richer children for full credit. (2 marks)

Summarising (2c). Choosing the main ideas from a longer text is the skill of summarising.

What this practises

Finding information (2b), Word meaning (2a), Explaining from the text, Inference (2d), Giving a view with evidence, Summarising (2c). The codes in brackets are the reading content domains from the national curriculum test framework. Marks and answers are a guide: accept any sensible answer that is backed up by the text.

Now try the writing task

Write a diary entry from a Victorian child on their first day at a new school or a new job. Show how the child feels, and use at least two sentences with a subordinate clause.

Aim for eight to twelve sentences. Read it back aloud together and fix anything that does not sound right.
